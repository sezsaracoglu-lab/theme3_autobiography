Autobiographical Memory

Recalling important events from a life. Built from your two set readings — Robinson-Riegler Chapter 7 (pp. 268–279) and Eysenck & Keane Chapter 8 (pp. 314–320) — and nothing else.
A note from your professor 🎓

Sezen — this problem looks like three unrelated topics sharing a chapter: why you cannot remember being two, why you remember being twenty so well, and a model of how memory is organised. It is not three topics. It is one argument.

The argument is this. Autobiographical memory is not a recording of your life. It is a structure built around a self — and because it is built around a self, it appears when a self appears, it favours the years in which that self was being decided, and when the self is in trouble the structure shows it. Childhood amnesia, the reminiscence bump, the self-memory system and the depression research are four views of that same claim.

How to use this platform. Go to Lessons and work through them in order — they build. In each lesson the diagram is the teaching: read it first, then the five points beside it. The gold box gives you the one sentence worth keeping. The green Memory hook is the version you will still have in April. Answer the comprehension questions out loud, not in your head — the difference is not small.

Then Flashcards for the terms and MCQ for exam practice. Lesson 15 is about depression and autobiographical memory; we study it because it is the strongest evidence that this model measures something real, but it is clinical material about real people's lives, and it is worth reading in that spirit.

L1

Two Kinds of Memory About You

Autobiographical memory vs. autobiographical fact — and why the border moves
🗂️
The same experience can start on the left and end on the right. Repetition is the engine that carries it across.
  • An autobiographical memory is a remembered experience — located in place and time, with sensory detail. It is a kind of episodic memory.
  • An autobiographical fact is a piece of self-knowledge with no particular occasion attached. It is a kind of semantic memory.
  • The distinction is not tidiness — the boundary between them moves. Experiences normally begin on the episodic side.
  • Linton (1975): repeated episodes lose their individualised character and become personalised semantic knowledge.
  • So you have hundreds of episodic memories of your first week at university and essentially none of your four-hundredth lecture.

★ The point

An autobiographical memory is an experience; an autobiographical fact is a piece of self-knowledge. The same event can begin as the first and end as the second — and repetition is what moves it.

🌿 Memory hook

Your phone's photo library. Individual photos are autobiographical memories. The album covers — "Rotterdam", "family", "that year" — are autobiographical facts. Look at a photo two hundred times and you stop seeing the morning and start seeing the album cover. That is Linton's drift.

  3. Nothing gets deleted in Linton's drift. So in what sense is the episode lost?
L2

How You Study a Life

Three methods, and the trade-off each one makes
🔬
The third method is the one to remember: dating each memory is what makes the retention function in Lesson 3 possible.
  • You cannot put a life in a laboratory, so all three methods are compromises.
  • Targeted event recall lets you choose corroborable events — which is how Usher & Neisser (Lesson 4) got round the accuracy problem.
  • Diary technique gives you ground truth, but writing an event down is rehearsal, so you have changed what you are measuring.
  • Galton–Crovitz is the oldest — it goes back to Galton — and was revived by Crovitz & Schiffman (1974).
  • Because cue-word memories get dated, you can count how many come from each age. That distribution is the autobiographical retention function.
Three ways to study a life — and what each one costs
METHOD 1
🎯 Targeted event recall
Ask about a named event. Simple — but you only learn about events you already thought to ask about.
METHOD 2
📓 Diary technique
Record events as they happen, test later. You control the encoding — at the price of years of work.
METHOD 3
🎣 Galton–Crovitz cue words
Give a word, take whatever memory comes, then ask when it happened. This is the method that produces the lifespan curve.

★ The point

Only one of the three methods lets memories arrive from the whole life span and get dated — and that is why the cue-word technique, not the other two, produced the curve the rest of this problem explains.

🌿 Memory hook

Three ways to survey a city. Targeted: you knock on four addresses you already chose. Diary: you give residents a logbook — accurate, but now they are watching themselves. Cue word: you shout a word in the square and see who comes out. You have no idea if they are telling the truth, but you finally learn which neighbourhoods people live in.

✓ Comprehension check
  1. You want to know whether people accurately remember their own childhood birthdays. Which method would you choose, and which would be a bad choice? One sentence each.
  2. Why does the diary technique contaminate the very thing it measures?
  3. Which single feature of the cue-word technique makes the retention function possible?
L3

The Shape of a Life

One curve, three phenomena — the autobiographical retention function
📈
Three separate research literatures live inside one line. Everything from here to Lesson 11 is an attempt to explain one of these three regions.
  • Rubin, Wetzler & Nebes (1986) used the cue-word technique on participants aged 18 to 76 and plotted memories by age.
  • Region 1 — childhood amnesia. Almost nothing before 3, few memories before 10.
  • Region 2 — the reminiscence bump. Disproportionately many memories from ages 10 to 30.
  • Region 3 — forgetting. An ordinary forgetting curve: the most recent years are recalled best.
  • The trap: region 3 is only clearly visible in the older participants. A 20-year-old has no "last twenty years" separate from their bump.

L4

Childhood Amnesia, and the Problem of Proof

The phenomenon, the methodological trap, and Usher & Neisser's way out
🧩
A provenance funnel, because the problem is a chain of custody: three routes in, one memory out, no receipt. Then the same rehearsal running two ways is the reversal that turns a methodological worry into a result.
  • Childhood amnesia: a precipitous drop in reported memories before age 10, and a complete absence before about age 2 or 3. Also called infantile amnesia.
  • "The emergence of autobiographical memory" and "the offset of childhood amnesia" mean exactly the same thing. Both readings switch between them without warning.
  • The proof problem: when an adult reports a memory from age four, there is no way to check they are remembering the event rather than the account of it.
  • Usher & Neisser's fix: targeted event recall on four documented events that relatives and records could confirm — a sibling's birth, a family move, a death in the family, a hospitalisation.
  • The reversal: rehearsal helped children who were 4–5 at the time and hurt those who were 3 or younger.

★ The point

The offset of childhood amnesia is not one date. It differs by the kind of event, and rehearsal does not simply strengthen an early memory — below about age four it can overwrite it.

🌿 Memory hook

Writing in wet cement. Before three the cement is liquid: press a hand in and the surface closes over, and if people keep walking across it — retelling, showing photographs — the print you did make is smeared out. By four or five it has started to set: now footprints stay, and going over the lines makes them clearer. Same rehearsal, opposite effect, because the material changed.

L5

So When Does the Amnesia Lift?

Know vs. recollect, and the wane at about 4.7 years
🔎
Schematic curves, not plotted data: the reading gives the endpoints (2–2.5 and ~5) and the 4.7-year mean, and says the transition is gradual. The crossing is the argument.
  • Published offsets ranged from 2–2.5 years (Eacott & Crawley, 1998; Usher & Neisser, 1998) to about 5 (Wetzler & Sweeney, 1986). Substantive disagreement, not sloppiness.
  • Multhaup et al. (2005) proposed the two camps were measuring different things, and had participants label each memory "know" or "recollect".
  • Know: I am aware the bicycle arrived on that birthday — I have seen the photograph. Recollect: I can re-experience cutting my hand on the gravel hill.
  • Childhood memories transition gradually from known to recollected; the point where the majority become recollect memories is the wane of childhood amnesia.
  • On average about 4.7 years — with considerable variation between individuals. Hold onto that variation; Lesson 8 turns it into evidence.

★ The point

The literature disagrees about the offset because "having a childhood memory" is two different things. Separate knowing about your childhood from re-experiencing it, and the disagreement turns into a gradual transition with a crossover at about 4.7 years.

🌿 Memory hook

A photograph album versus a film reel. Early on, your childhood is all album — you can point at it and say what happened, but nothing moves. Somewhere around four or five the reel starts running, and you can sit inside the scene again. Nobody flipped a switch; the album slowly turned into film.

L6

Why It Lifts, Account 1 & 2: Brain and Language

Immature hardware — and the objection that sinks it. Then narrative style and memory talk
🧠
Two tracks on one age axis, because both accounts say "memory waits for X to mature" — and the red pins are where each claim runs out of room.
  • Account 1 — brain development. The hippocampal areas critical for forming long-term memories are underdeveloped in the young; prefrontal cortex develops rapidly around age 1.
  • It fits the adult literature: hippocampal patients cannot store recent events yet retain skills — memory is several subsystems, not one.
  • But children do not show childhood amnesia. A 2–3-year-old can report things from when they were 1–2 (Bauer, 2004; Bauer et al., 2000) — so the basic machinery was in place, and immature hardware cannot be the whole story.
  • Account 2 — language. Pillemer & White (1989): children begin to remember events as soon as they can describe them. Narrative style matters (Fivush, 1991; Reese & Fivush, 1993) — elaborative beats pragmatic, and Davis (1999) found a female advantage in AM recall.
  • Nelson (1993): AM emerges as parents engage in memory talk — play-by-play announcers of what happened, how and why — building generic event memory for repeats and AM for one-offs.

★ The point

A three-year-old can recall being eighteen months old, yet that same person at twenty cannot. If it could be retrieved at three, the machinery was already there — so childhood amnesia is not a hardware problem.

🌿 Memory hook

Brain account: the camera hadn't been built. But the toddler's photos prove a camera existed. Language account: the camera worked, but nobody had the captions — and without a caption, nothing survives the filing. The obvious next question is whether captions make the photo or merely let you find it.

L7

Why It Lifts, Account 3: The Cognitive Self

Howe & Courage, Wang's cross-cultural test, and Jack et al.'s ten-year follow-up
🪞
The dots are schematic — the reading reports a strong negative correlation, not individual scores. The line is the finding: the more a mother elaborated when the child was 2–4, the earlier that child’s first memory reached back ten years later.
  • Howe & Courage (1997): language is critical to the expression of stored experience, but it is not the same as the experience stored. An 18-month-old may have a sophisticated notion of yesterday without the words for it.
  • Their candidate is the developing sense of self — and the logic is clean: if AM is knowledge of oneself and one's experience, children cannot show it until there is a self for the experience to belong to.
  • Wang (2006) tested this by varying the kind of self. Results exactly as predicted: US participants had earlier memories for every cue word, and their memories reflected personal autonomy more for every cue word.
  • This does not mean relationships are unimportant in the West — it means the relative weight of autonomy and relationships differs. Memories cued by "mother" gave especially early remembering.
  • Jack et al. (2009), first prospective test: a strong negative correlation between the mother's elaboration-to-repetition ratio and the age of the earliest memory ten years later.

Why It Lifts, Account 4: Social-Cognitive

The account that absorbs the other three — and turns the disagreement into a prediction
🎭

  • Nelson & Fivush (2004) take the word emergence seriously: assuming a barrier that, once removed, permits memories to form is overly simplistic.
  • Their requirements: basic explicit memory (in place by age 2, Bauer 2004) · language · a developing understanding of narrative · extended consciousness (≈ age 4, Povinelli et al., 1999) · a theory of mind.
  • Extended consciousness = the sense that your present self is connected to a past self. Theory of mind = understanding you have beliefs and knowledge others cannot access.
  • The child begins to encode episodes as part of a coherent, meaningful story — the beginning story of their own life.
  • Why this account wins: if several factors must coalesce, and they coalesce in different orders at different rates, there should not be a single offset age. The literature's disagreement is what this theory predicts.

The Reminiscence Bump

The obvious answer, and the four findings that break it
✨

Three Accounts of the Bump

Memory mechanisms · identity formation · peak functioning — and Janssen & Murre's awkward result
⚖️
A scorecard, because one study adjudicates between three candidates. The middle column is what kills rows 1 and 2: both of them need the events to be special.
  • 1 · Memory mechanisms. Ordinary mechanisms favour early adulthood: frequent rehearsal because the events are important, low interference because they are distinctive.
  • 2 · Identity formation. Erikson (1950): those years are the critical period for identity. Habermas & Bluck (2000), Getting a Life: both the cognitive tools for a life story and the motives for having one develop in adolescence.
  • The elegant parallel: childhood events become memorable once we have a sense that we are; later events once we have a sense of exactly who we are. Same logic, one level up.
  • 3 · Peak functioning. Cognitive abilities and brain function peak in early adulthood, so whatever is experienced then is simply encoded better.
  • Janssen & Murre (2008) found a clear bump in memories rated unemotional, neutral and unimportant — everything that should make them forgettable.

📖How they reconcile it with the intuitive accounts

Janssen and Murre propose that in older participants, the more novel, emotional and important events get rehearsed at the expense of the mundane ones, so with increasing age those memories win out. Everyday events from the reminiscence period may enjoy an advantage while we are still relatively close to adolescence, and that advantage fades with age. On this reading, identity formation and the cognitive account do not explain the bump itself — they explain the different forgetting rates for mundane versus novel, emotional and important events.

⚠️ Memorise this comparison — it is the kind of precision an exam likes

Brain development is necessary but not sufficient to explain why the amnesia of childhood lifts. It appears to be both necessary and sufficient to explain the reminiscence bump. Cognitive factors may explain which things we remember from that period; neurological functioning may explain why we remember them at all.

📎 One more account, on the edge of your extract

The life-script account is already in progress on p. 314, the first page of the Eysenck & Keane extract. The bump is much stronger for positive memories than negative ones, which supports a culturally shared script of expected life events guiding retrieval. But the late teens and twenties also differ from surrounding periods in other ways — the establishment of self-identity, and a concentration of novel, first-time experiences — so several factors likely contribute. Eysenck and Keane note one could equally argue the strongest memories are those most relevant to self-identity (Conway, 2005), which develops substantially in adolescence and early adulthood.

★ The point

Strip out novelty, emotion, valence and importance, and the bump is still there. So the bump is not made of significant events — significance only decides which of that period's events survive into old age.

🌿 Memory hook

The camera was at its best in those years, so everything came out sharp — including things not worth photographing. Then rehearsal acts like an album: over the decades you keep reprinting the dramatic shots and lose the ordinary ones. By seventy the window looks like it was made of milestones. It wasn't; the rest just wore away.

Forgetting the Rest of a Life

An ordinary forgetting curve, three dull causes, and one cultural twist
🌫️
The chapter opened with the memory/fact distinction. Forgetting turns out to be one of the engines that moves material across it.
  • Wagenaar (1986): memory dropped from nearly 75% correct to less than 33% over a four-year period — the same shape found in countless studies since Ebbinghaus's classic self-study.
  • Cause 1 — lack of rehearsal. Probably the most common. Unrehearsed events leave a representation that is at best transient, at worst nonexistent.
  • Cause 2 — interference. Think of a month of events and how hard it is to retrieve one against the others. Most events simply do not stand out.
  • Cause 3 — routinisation. Regular days blend together, and Linton (1975) returns: repeated episodes lose their individualised character and become autobiographical facts.
  • Wang (2009); Wang & Ross (2007): culture also varies the specificity of what survives — Euro-American cultures tend towards specific episodes, Asian and Asian-American cultures towards generic memories.

The Self-Memory System

Conway & Pleydell-Pearce (2000) and Conway (2005) — the architecture, and coherence vs correspondence
🏛️
Two components, not one: the hierarchy on the left, the working self on the right. Almost every exam question turns on the relationship between them.
  • Component 1 — the knowledge base. Original three levels: lifetime periods (months–years, defined by an ongoing situation) → general events (repeated and single) → event-specific knowledge (images and feelings, seconds to hours, in correct temporal order).
  • Component 2 — the working self. Concerned with the self, what it may become, and your current goals. Its goals decide what is stored and what is recalled.
  • The consequence, in their own words: autobiographical memories are primarily "records of success or failure in goal attainment" (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000, p. 266).
  • Conway (2005) additions. Knowledge divided into the conceptual self and episodic memories (previously event-specific knowledge); and two new levels on top — the life story and themes (work, relationships).
  • Coherence vs correspondence. We want memories to fit our goals and beliefs (coherence) and to be accurate (correspondence). Over time, coherence tends to win.

Two Routes In, and How the Model Holds Up

Generative vs direct retrieval, the supporting evidence, and the three limitations
🚪
A map, because the model is about routing: one route detours through the working self and picks up its goals on the way; the other goes straight past it. Woike et al. (1999) is the check that the detour is real — agentic and communal people produced systematically different memories to the same request.
  • Generative retrieval: deliberately constructing a memory by combining the working self's resources with the knowledge base. Memories often relate to the individual's goals.
  • Direct retrieval: does not involve the working self. Triggered spontaneously by specific cues — hearing "Paris" on the radio may trigger a holiday there. Less effortful, less active involvement of the rememberer.
  • Uzer et al. (2012): participants recalled memories to emotion words (frustrated, amused) while thinking aloud. Direct retrieval was three times faster and involved much less vocalisation.
  • Johannessen & Berntsen (2010): directly retrieved memories are more specific, less significant and less relevant to personal identity — as predicted, since the working self and goals were more involved in generative retrieval.
  • Woike et al. (1999): the working self really governs retrieval. Agentic and communal people produced systematically different memories of the same request.

📖The uncomfortable corollary: coherence really does win

Conway (2005) argued autobiographical memories are often inaccurate because we want them to be consistent with the working self's goals. The demonstration: people often claim aspects of their lives — the self, a marital relationship — are better now than in the past. But this "improvement" is typically due to misremembering their past selves in a negatively biased way (see Newman & Lindsay, 2009, for a review). The past is revised downwards so the present can be an improvement. And the reading does not moralise: such inaccuracies can enhance our self-image and our social relationships. The system is not malfunctioning — it is doing the job the model says it is for.

✔ Strengths✘ Limitations
Conway & Pleydell-Pearce (2000) and Conway (2005) provide a reasonably comprehensive account of autobiographical memoryFirst: retrieval involves several brain areas and processes, and the model does not capture all this complexity
Several major theoretical assumptions are well supported: the hierarchical structure; the intimate relationship between autobiographical memory and the self; the importance of goalsSecond: we need to know more about how the working self interacts with the knowledge base to produce recall of specific memories
There is good support for the distinction between generative and direct retrievalThird: memories vary in how much episodic information (contextual details) and semantic information (world knowledge) they contain, and this is not addressed fully within the model
These limitations are not throwaway criticisms — the first sets up Lesson 14, and the third is the episodic/semantic boundary from Lesson 1 arriving back as an open question.
★ The point

Generative and direct retrieval differ in one thing that then produces every other difference: whether the working self is in the loop. Speed, effort, specificity and identity-relevance all follow from that single fact.

🌿 Memory hook

Generative is searching your own filing cabinet: you start from the question, walk down through the drawers, and assemble an answer. Slow, deliberate, and because you chose the question, the answer comes out shaped like something that matters to you. Direct is the smell of a particular cleaning product in a stairwell: a drawer flew open by itself, and what is in your hand is oddly specific and not especially meaningful — a Tuesday afternoon, fully rendered, for no reason.

What Cognitive Neuroscience Adds

Four networks, two jobs — and the same control trick used twice
🔬
Put the four networks on a head and they stop being a list. Svoboda et al. (2006) is the meta-analysis behind the medial and ventromedial prefrontal involvement; the amygdala sits in the temporal lobe and matters for the emotional ones.
  • Svoboda et al. (2006), meta-analysis: the medial and ventromedial prefrontal cortex were nearly always activated during autobiographical retrieval, as were medial and lateral temporal cortex.
  • Summerfield et al. (2009): participants produced real and imagined events. Only recall of real events activated the ventromedial PFC and the posterior cingulate cortex.
  • The amygdala, buried in the temporal lobe, is strongly associated with emotion — so we would expect it here. Among medial-temporal patients, those also with amygdala damage find emotional autobiographical memories harder to retrieve (Buchanan et al., 2006). Markowitsch & Staniloiu (2011): amygdala activation for autobiographical retrieval but not for fictitious material.
  • St Jacques et al. (2011) identified four networks: fronto-parietal (adaptive controlled processes, probably verbal retrieval) · cingulo-operculum (goal maintenance) · medial PFC (self-referential processing) · medial temporal lobe (declarative memory, conscious recollection).
  • Two extra points the reading singles out: strong bi-directional connections, so none operates in isolation; and all four do the initial construction, but only medial PFC and medial temporal lobes do the subsequent elaboration.

When the System Goes Wrong

Over-general memory in depression, and the measured structure of a life story
🕯️
📖The full explanation

There are clear-cut individual differences in autobiographical memory, and research in this area has focused on patients with major depressive disorder, or on healthy individuals high in depression. There is a reason this belongs in this problem rather than a clinical one. It is assumed within the self-memory system model that the information stored in autobiographical memory reflects the individual's personality and their sense of self. If that is right, the two should be visible in each other — and our understanding of depression can be increased by considering in detail how autobiographical memory differs in depressed and non-depressed individuals.

Read the two studies as a pair. Each on its own is suggestive; together they pin down the direction.
  • Over-general memory: depressed individuals show reduced autobiographical memory specificity — given the cue angry, a depressed person might say "Arguing with other people" (see Liu et al., 2013, for a meta-analysis).
  • In the vocabulary of Lesson 1, that answer is an autobiographical fact, not an autobiographical memory.
  • Stange et al. (2013), prospective: over-generality predicted increases in depressive symptoms eight months later in Caucasian adolescents exposed to high levels of familial emotional abuse — a vulnerability, not merely a symptom.
  • Brittlebank et al. (1993), longitudinal: increased specificity predicted recovery from major depressive disorder.
  • Affect regulation hypothesis: a cognitive avoidance strategy — staying vague keeps painful specifics out of reach. Debeer et al. (2011): threat instructions increased over-generality only among individuals high in avoidant coping. The reading immediately adds: not that all depressed individuals have avoidant coping strategies.

Three measures run one way; positive redundancy runs the other. And on most measures the remitted group sits between the other two.
  • The striking null: all three groups identified between nine and ten lifetime periods on average. The number of life chapters is not what differs.
  • Proportion negative was much greater for depressed patients than controls.

Dalgleish et al. (2011): the architecture of a life, in three groups.
  1. The organisation of autobiographical knowledge in currently depressed patients is revealing concerning their working self as described by Conway and Pleydell-Pearce (2000). More generally, their perceived self is revealed in the predominantly negative and non-integrated structure of their autobiographical knowledge.
  2. The structure is more integrated and less pervasively negative in patients in remission than in current patients — suggesting part of recovery involves having a "healthier" perspective on one's life history.
  3. Patients in remission nevertheless had a more negative and less integrated view of their life histories than healthy controls. These findings suggest these patients were at risk of a subsequent depressive episode.
📖The full explanation

Read conclusions 2 and 3 together, because that is where the real content is. Remission is not restoration. The life story of someone in remission has improved and has not returned to the control pattern — and the residue is not incidental, it is a risk marker. A structural feature of how a life is organised in memory carries prognostic information.

★ The point

All three groups divided their lives into nine or ten chapters. What differed was not how much life there was, but how negative each chapter was, and whether anything good ran through more than one of them.

🌿 Memory hook

A life story as a book of about ten chapters — and everyone, depressed or not, writes about ten. In the control version certain good things recur: the same warmth, the same competence, the same few people turn up in chapter two and chapter seven. That recurrence is positive redundancy, and it is the thread that makes ten chapters one book. In the currently depressed version the good things do not carry over: each chapter is sealed off (compartmentalisation) and more of what is in it is negative. Remission re-threads some of it — and what is still unthreaded is what puts the person at risk of a further episode.

Synthesis

One paragraph that ties the whole problem together
🧵
★ The thesis

Autobiographical memory is not a record of a life but an instrument of a self: it comes into existence when a child has a self for experiences to belong to, it over-represents the two decades in which that self was being settled, it is organised into a hierarchy that a goal-directed working self searches from the top down, it sacrifices accuracy to consistency whenever the two conflict — and when the self is disordered, the structure of the remembered life is disordered in exactly the ways the model predicts.

📖The full explanation
The spine of the whole problem. Everything else hangs off one of these five claims.
📖Now watch the thesis do the work

Start at the beginning of a life. If autobiographical memory required only working neural hardware, a three-year-old could not recall being eighteen months old — and yet they can, which means childhood amnesia cannot be explained by immature machinery alone. What is missing in the first years is not storage but a subject: Howe and Courage's cognitive self, the thing an experience has to belong to before it counts as yours. Wang's cross-cultural work is the cleanest evidence, because varying the kind of self a culture builds changes both when the earliest memories start and what they are about. The social-cognitive account then adds that a self is not the only requirement — which is why nobody can name a single offset age, and why the variability in Multhaup et al. is a prediction rather than an embarrassment.

Move to the middle of a life, and the same logic reappears one level up. Childhood events become memorable once we have a sense that we are; the events of adolescence and early adulthood become memorable once we are working out who we are. That is the identity-formation account, and Habermas and Bluck supply the mechanism. But Rubin, Rahhal and Poon block any purely identity-based explanation, because the bump also covers song preferences and World Series results — and Janssen and Murre push harder still, finding a bump in memories rated unemotional, neutral and unimportant. So the window is privileged for reasons partly neurological and partly biographical, with rehearsal deciding which of the period's events survive into old age.

Now turn from the phenomena to the architecture, and the thesis becomes the theory. Conway and Pleydell-Pearce give autobiographical memory a hierarchy and a working self whose goals determine what is stored and recalled. The evidence that this is two systems is everywhere: retrograde amnesic patients lose the bottom of the hierarchy while keeping the top, and KC has no episodic memories at all yet retains general knowledge of his own life. The evidence that the working self governs retrieval is Woike's agentic and communal participants producing systematically different memories of the same request. And the evidence that memory is in the self's service rather than the truth's is that people experience their lives as improving largely by misremembering their past selves as worse than they were. Coherence beats correspondence — not a malfunction but the design.

The two retrieval routes then fall out of the model rather than being added to it. If the working self does the searching, bypassing it should give memories that are faster, less effortful, more specific and less identity-relevant — exactly what Uzer, Johannessen and Berntsen, and Addis report. Cognitive neuroscience adds the stage structure the model was missing: all four networks are needed to find a memory, but only self-reference and declarative recollection stay to fill it in.

Finally, the clinical evidence closes the argument, because it is the strongest test available. If autobiographical memory really is built around a self, then a damaged sense of self should show up as a damaged structure — not merely as sad content. It does. And Dalgleish et al. find the structural signature: all three groups divide their lives into nine or ten chapters, so the quantity of life is not what differs. What differs is that in currently depressed patients the chapters are more negative and sealed off from one another with no positive thread between them; that in remission the thread is partly restored; and that the portion still missing in remission marks a risk of relapse.

So: a system built around a self begins when the self begins, privileges the years the self was decided, is searched by the self's current goals, bends the past to fit the self's story, and falls apart in the shape of the self's own disorder. Every study in this problem is a measurement of one of those five claims.
